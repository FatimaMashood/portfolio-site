## Exhibition overview

The Rijksmuseum Boerhaave, in Leiden, showcases the intersectionality of the disciplines of art and science where the medical collection displays the nuances of the intersectionality, either through the technique curated for the specimen by the Albinus brothers, or the anatomical theatre and its historical significance against Galen's teachings of the time.

## Collections at the museum

Among the diverse exhibits, a few exceptional collections by different artists/scientists were particularly interesting to observe.

Specimens by the Albinus brothers, 18th-century anatomists, presented wet and dry anatomical samples and medical instruments such as forceps. In 1725 Bernard Siegfried Albinus made life-like wet preparations showcased at the Museum Boerhaave, Leiden. The technique used to create such specimens allowed Albinus to preserve parts of these individuals. This also puts into perspective the debate around consent in human anatomy or lack thereof at the time. While observing the specimen, I was intrigued by the narrative behind the dramatics; the use of fabric, size and proportionality centring the central specimen in the jars.

  <img src="/images/elsewhere/exhibit-boerhaave-manikin-1200.webp" alt="A worn chamois leather obstetric training manikin lying in a glass display case." loading="lazy" />

Another interesting part of the collection was the Obstetric Manikin full of secrets. This artefact represents the chamois leather training manikin, used to teach midwifery in the 18th century. It was recently discovered that the artefact incorporated skeletal parts in the woman's and doll's torsos, observed through medical tests such as CT scans. While men midwifery anatomy was not a glorified field, individuals such as William Smellie and William Hunter, men midwives of the 18th century, published illustrations of the pregnant female body with unique means of representation, stepping away from the dominant focus on male anatomy by giving the pregnant female body exclusive focus.

The immense focus placed on accuracy and realism perpetuates the ideas put forth by the 16th-century anatomist responsible for the first anatomical book based on first-hand dissections of human cadavers, Andreas Vesalius.

<figure class="inline-figure is-wide">
  <img src="/images/elsewhere/exhibit-boerhaave-prints-1200.webp" alt="A dark gallery wall of framed anatomical prints, including a portrait of Andreas Vesalius, skeletons and a dissection scene." loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption>Anatomical prints, including a portrait of Andreas Vesalius, Rijksmuseum Boerhaave</figcaption>
</figure>

Vesalius, prominently critical of Galen's teachings, introduced a tradition where anatomists based their illustrations on their perception/observation. He often led dissections at the anatomical theatre and published some of his work from the dissections performed. The anatomical theatre at the museum represented a fanciful representation that focused less on representing the importance of the vigour with which Vesalius led dissections and lessons for students and viewers.

With the many specimens at the museum, these three artefacts were particularly interesting for me because of their narrative and historical significance to medicine in Europe, and perpetuate the importance of narrative representation of specimens at museums.
